Audio-Conditioned Text Generation

Research documentation from the discussion about using music and audio as an active conditioning signal for AI-generated text rather than treating audio merely as speech to be transcribed.

1. Core Research Question

Traditional audio-to-text systems primarily answer: “What words were spoken?”

This research direction asks: “How does the audio itself influence what the AI writes?”

For music, useful information may include tempo, rhythm, harmony, instrumentation, timbre, dynamics, spectral characteristics, intensity, and changes over time. These properties could influence narrative mood, pacing, sentence structure, imagery, vocabulary, tension, and other properties of generated prose.

Key distinction: This is not simply music-to-caption generation. The desired system uses music as a conditioning signal that changes the generation process.

2. Conceptual Pipeline

Audio Input Music, soundtrack, ambience, speech, or other recorded audio.
Audio Representation Convert the signal into measurable features or a learned embedding.
Cross-Modal Interpretation Relate acoustic information to semantic, emotional, stylistic, or narrative concepts.
Language Conditioning Use the resulting representation to influence an LLM.
Generated Text The same story seed can produce different prose under different audio conditions.

3. Major Technical Approaches

Feature Extraction + Prompt Conditioning

Use tools such as librosa or essentia to extract BPM, spectral centroid, RMS energy, key, spectral variance and related features. Map them into writing controls such as cadence, intensity, imagery and vocabulary density.

Audio Embedding / Latent Steering

Encode audio into a learned vector representation. CLAP-style audio-text representations can be compared with textual concepts or prose-style descriptors, potentially avoiding a small hand-written set of mood labels.

Native Audio-Conditioned LLMs

Use a multimodal language model that accepts audio directly, then instruct it to treat musical properties as an influence on narrative generation rather than simply producing a transcript.

4. Audio Properties That Could Influence Writing

  • Tempo / BPM: narrative speed and sentence cadence.
  • Rhythm: repetition, phrasing and prose rhythm.
  • Energy / loudness: intensity and escalation.
  • Spectral brightness: atmosphere and imagery.
  • Harmony / key: tonal or emotional direction.
  • Timbre: sonic character and texture.
  • Instrumentation: contextual or stylistic evidence.
  • Dynamic changes: transitions, tension, release and climax.
  • Temporal structure: lets text respond to changes in a soundtrack.

5. Dynamic Music-to-Narrative Conditioning

Instead of reducing a whole track to one mood label, the system can analyze the soundtrack over time.

Low-energy / sparse section Establish atmosphere.
Increasing rhythm / density Build anticipation.
Crescendo Increase narrative tension or action.
Sudden reduction Release, reflection or tonal reversal.

The important idea is that musical structure becomes a temporal control signal. The model does not have to describe the music. The music can influence how the story unfolds and how the prose behaves.

6. CLAP-Style Cross-Modal Experiment

A shared audio-text embedding can be used to compare an audio representation against textual descriptions of narrative qualities.

Audio
  ↓
Audio Encoder
  ↓
Audio Embedding
  ↓
Compare against textual style / narrative embeddings
  ↓
Weighted influence profile
  ↓
LLM conditioning
  ↓
Narrative text

Possible textual reference dimensions include melancholic, surreal, comic, ominous, dreamlike, urgent, peaceful, chaotic, rhythmic, and minimalist.

The experimental question is whether learned cross-modal representations can produce useful steering richer than a small manually designed list of mood labels.

7. Native Audio Models

Potential experimentation can involve audio-capable multimodal language models such as Qwen Audio-family models, as well as audio representation systems. Model capabilities, current releases, APIs, licensing and hardware requirements should be verified before implementation.

9. Simplest Proof of Concept

A first experiment does not require training a new model. Analyze the audio, convert the measurements into a dynamic conditioning prompt, and compare the output with a no-audio baseline.

audio = load_audio("scene_music.wav")
features = analyze_audio(audio)
conditioning = map_features_to_writing_controls(features)

prompt = f"""
Write the scene using the supplied story premise.

Musical conditioning:
{conditioning}

Allow the music to influence:
- pacing
- sentence cadence
- emotional intensity
- imagery
- vocabulary density
- scene tension

Do not describe the music unless the story requires it.
Use the music as an invisible influence on the writing.
"""

generate_story(prompt)

10. Controlled Experiment

Hold the story prompt constant and change only the audio condition. Compare sentence length, lexical diversity, emotional language, pacing, event density and human-rated alignment with the soundtrack.

11. Research Questions

  1. Can music influence generated prose without the model explicitly describing the music?
  2. Are learned audio embeddings more effective than manually extracted musical features?
  3. Can changes in a soundtrack control changes in narrative tension over time?
  4. Can audio influence sentence cadence as well as semantic content?
  5. Can the same fictional character behave differently under different musical conditions?
  6. Can music become one evidence stream inside a larger fictional behavior engine?
  7. Can audio interact with transcript, image, character, relationship and world-state evidence?

In this architecture, a transcript can provide explicit content while the soundtrack provides additional information about perceived tension, atmosphere, rhythm or tonal direction. Audio therefore becomes another evidence stream rather than merely another transcription source.

13. Central Research Insight

Audio → representation → influence → language

rather than:

Audio → transcript → language

The broader implication is a multimodal generation system in which music, sound design, images, dialogue and other signals can each influence generation through different computational channels.
